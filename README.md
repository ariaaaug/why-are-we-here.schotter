# why-are-we-here.schotter
In 2026, large language models and intelligent code assistants can transform natural language prompts into functional code within seconds. A single sentence can render a complex visual grid or construct an interactive canvas. For a design student navigating this landscape, a fundamental question emerges: if machines can write functional code on demand, why should designers still learn to program? The answer lies not in competing with machines over syntax speed, but in acquiring the precise agency required to convert vague creative intent into meaningful artistic expression.

## Describing vs. Specifying: Lessons from a Marine Interaction
The core limitation of natural language lies in the subtle divide between describing an experience and specifying it. Anyone can describe a design vision using evocative adjectives, but computers require absolute logical parameters to execute it.
Consider a simple interactive sketch I created: a school of marine creatures following cursor movement and releasing bubbles upon interaction. Communicating this concept to a modern generative system is effortless: "Create an interactive ocean scene where fish follow the mouse and produce bubbles." The system will produce working code instantly. However, the result inevitably relies on generic, mechanical defaults.
An AI model cannot feel or guess the exact emotional rhythm of the interaction. It does not know whether the distance threshold for triggering bubbles should be 50 pixels or 100 pixels—a subtle numeric difference that determines whether the creature feels responsive or overly jumpy. Nor can it automatically tune the random probability governing bubble frequency to make the aquatic life feel genuinely organic rather than like an automated dispenser. While natural language is inherently ambiguous, code is exact. The vitality of an interactive experience lives within micro-adjustments of numerical variables. Without understanding code, a designer remains a passive consumer of whatever arbitrary defaults a system provides.

## Code as Material and the Question of Authorship
Designers have always defined their mastery through an intimate understanding of their medium. Painters understand the fluidity and viscosity of oil pigments; photographers understand the behavior of optical light. In computational art, logical structures, conditional statements, event listeners, and variable scopes form the foundational material of the craft.
As philosopher Don Ihde (1990) argued in his phenomenology of technology, technological tools are never neutral instruments; they actively reshape human perception and mediate how we experience the world (p. 21). When designers treat code as an impenetrable black box, their creative capacity is bounded by the pre-configured imaginations of software developers or machine learning training sets. Understanding the underlying logic grants agency over the medium, transforming code from a technical obstacle into an expressive material that can be dismantled, debugged, and restructured at will.
Furthermore, the fear that automated systems will render human artists obsolete is a historical repetition rather than an unprecedented crisis. When Georg Nees and Frieder Nake pioneered computer-generated art in the 1960s, critics similarly argued that plotting machines would displace human creative intuition (Nees, 1969). Yet, history demonstrates that technological automation rarely eliminates art; instead, it elevates the locus of human agency.
When routine production tasks are automated, the artist's role shifts toward structural decision-making, critical evaluation, and authorship. The artistic value of an interactive piece resides not in the physical labor of typing syntax line by line, but in the deliberate choices made during the design process—the threshold calibrated, the rhythm tuned, and the intent enforced.

## Conclusion
We do not learn to program in the era of artificial intelligence to outpace machines at generating syntax. We learn to program to retain the capacity to interrogate complex systems, refine subtle details, and maintain genuine authorship over our work. AI assistants can write functional code on command, but only designers possess the critical intuition to specify meaning and soul.
